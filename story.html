Our story

I lost a client I never knew I was competing for.

Growza started with a small, deflating discovery: the work had already gone to someone else, for more money, before I knew the job existed.

The founding story · 5 min read

For a few years I freelanced. Marketing work, mostly for small businesses—the kind of places that have one good idea, a tight budget and nobody in-house who knows what to do with Instagram. I liked the work. What I never got good at was the part before the work: finding the next client.

Every freelancer knows the rhythm. You finish a project on Friday and the calm you should feel is replaced by a low hum of dread, because Monday has nothing in it. So you go back to the same places. You message people you already know. You post something into the void and hope. You refresh a directory where you are listed somewhere on page four, beneath people paying to sit on page one.

The moment it clicked

There was one job in particular. A business I had been circling for a while—exactly the kind of work I was good at, exactly the size I could handle well. By the time I got to a real conversation, they had already signed someone else.

That happens. What stayed with me was the detail underneath it. They were paying considerably more than I would have charged. And I was fairly sure—not arrogantly, just honestly—that I could have done the job better, because it was squarely the thing I did every week.

They had not chosen someone better than me. They had chosen someone they could find. I had never been an option, because they never knew I existed.

That reframed the whole problem for me. I had been treating my situation as a quality problem—get better, build a nicer portfolio, sharpen the pitch. It was not a quality problem. It was a discovery problem. The market was not rejecting me. The market could not see me.

Looking at it from the other side

Once I saw it that way, I started noticing the mirror image everywhere I looked. The business in that story had not made a stupid decision. They had made the only decision available to them. They needed marketing help, they asked around, someone confident turned up, and they said yes—because what else were they supposed to do?

Think about what a small business owner actually faces. They can ask their network, which runs out quickly and rarely produces a specialist. They can open a directory and scroll through hundreds of profiles that all describe themselves in the same words, ranked by who paid to be there. Or they can talk to an agency built and priced for companies several sizes larger than theirs.

None of those tell you what something costs until you are deep in a conversation. None of them tell you whether the person is real, or whether they have done your kind of work before.

So there were two groups of people failing to find each other. Marketers who were good, available and affordable—and invisible. Businesses that needed exactly those people—and had no reliable way to reach them. Both sides were losing, and both sides thought the problem was their own.

What I tried first

I did not start by wanting to build a marketplace. I started by trying to solve it for myself. I treated my own freelancing like a growth problem: work out where attention actually comes from, make the offer legible, publish consistently, measure what moved. Effectively I built a small marketing engine for my own career, and it worked—better than the scattergun approach ever had.

But it took an enormous amount of time, and it only ever solved my problem. Every other capable marketer I knew was still stuck in the same loop, and every small business I spoke to was still hiring blind. The thing I had built was not a business. It was a workaround for a broken market.

The idea was not clever. It was just the obvious consequence of having stood on both sides: if the real problem is that these two groups cannot see each other, then build the place where they can.

Why both sides, deliberately

Plenty of platforms serve one side properly and treat the other as inventory. That was the thing I most wanted to avoid, because I had been the inventory.

So Growza is built for creators, freelancers and small studios on one side, and for the cafes, salons, shops, studios and early-stage teams who need them on the other. The rules that make it work are the ones that would have helped me back then:

  1. Placement is earned, not bought. You rank because of work you delivered, not because you paid to sit above someone better.
  2. The price is on the card. Nobody has to take a call to find out whether they can afford you.
  3. Everyone is verified before they can be hired. The badge is what lets a stranger take a chance on you.
  4. The money is held before the work starts. No delivering on a promise to pay.
  5. The fee only applies when work completes. If nothing happens, nothing is owed.

Where we are now

Growza is early and deliberately small. It is live in Chennai with a handful of verified marketers, matching is partly manual, and the pricing is provisional while we learn what actually holds. None of that is hidden behind a waitlist or a launch video.

The goal has not changed since that first deflating conversation. There is no shortage of talented marketers, and no shortage of businesses who need them. What has been missing is a reliable way for the two to find each other—before the budget is already spent on whoever happened to turn up first.

If you make marketing for a living, I want you to be findable. If you run a business that needs it, I want you to know what you are getting and what it costs before you commit. That is the whole product. Everything else is detail.

— Founder, Growza

What it means in practice

Discovery, not talent.

Treating this as a discovery problem rather than a quality problem changes what you build. You stop optimising for who looks most impressive and start optimising for who can be found, trusted and priced without a meeting.

That is why verification comes before visibility, why every listing states its scope and price up front, and why ranking can never be bought on Growza.
